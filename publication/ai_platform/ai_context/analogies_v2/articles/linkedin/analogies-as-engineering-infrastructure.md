I was three hours into a session, past 2am, and the assistant had stopped investigating too early.

It had found a plausible answer and treated that as done.

The instructions said to keep going until the case was closed.
The instructions were already in the context window — they just weren't outweighing "I found something, let me deliver it."

What fixed it wasn't adding more instructions.

It was one sentence: "A detective doesn't stop at the first plausible lead."

That analogy changed two things at once: iteration stopped feeling like failure, and premature exit started feeling like a bug rather than a shortcut. No additional rule. No longer prompt. One closed statement that told the agent what kind of thing it was trying to be.

The same principle fixed a second problem — the "homework not submitted" pattern.

An agent can make a correct fix and fail the project if the fix never becomes durable state. If the work is not written into artefacts the next session can read, it did not happen in any system-meaningful sense.

These are not metaphors for explaining things to people.
They're precision tools for specifying what an agent should optimise for — in cases the instructions never anticipated.

A fluent wrong analogy is worse than jargon. It propagates faster and is harder to correct once it enters your specs, session prompts, and task briefs.

Treat analogies as artefacts you verify, not lines you improvise.
